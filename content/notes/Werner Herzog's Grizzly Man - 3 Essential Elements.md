The *Grizzly Man* (2005) is Werner Herzog's over an hour-long documentary that shows one's journey to blend into the wild and bond with the bears, or at least an attempt to. The film's main focus is Timothy Treadwell; he is an American environmentalist, filmmaker, and founder of the Grizzly People organization who lived among wild coastal brown bears in some of Alaska's National Parks for 13 summers. This film progresses through the story of Treadwell's passion–some may call it obsession–with grizzly bears and his view toward the interaction between humans and animals, all while including Herzog's interviews with people in Treadwell's social circle, with Herzog's own opinion-based narration throughout the film. This projects to the audience of how there can be different perspectives in Treadwell's decision: accepted, solemn, well-deserved, or even intensely aversive. The director, Werner Herzog, is famous for ambitious main characters with individuals in conflict with nature–which sometimes can develop into the point of insanity. This film is composed of three primary elements: visualization, emotional values, and variation of viewpoints. Despite being broad in its categories, it helps explain the film's allure to the audience.

What is considered to be most captivating in this film is the use of visual elements of both filmmakers: Treadwell and Herzog. The audience can identify the transition or the difference between the two due to the quality of the recording; this is one of the reflections of the two filmmakers despite sharing the same profession. Treadwell's footage is understandable for his limited ability to capture everything by himself, even though later on in the film it is shown that in one or two pieces of his footage was assisted by Treadwell's girlfriend, Amie Huguenard. On the contrary, Herzog's professional team has shown the audience the stark contrast between the quality of the camera's lens as it is used to take the scenery in the more advanced angle–shooting the bird-eye view from the helicopter of the glacier. The difference between the two technologies does not undermine Treadwell's inferior capture device; instead, it enhances the experience through his own "hand-made" documentary and captivates his passion into spreading the message to the world. Treadwell did so effectively in some of his footage that show the scenery of the magnificent mountain in Alaska with the bears thus showing to the audience his capacity to set up and organize his own shot independently in spite of having a lower quality digital camera. It has to be thought thoroughly for Treadwell's attempt to take a shot with the bear in the background, as he narrated his own documentary was extremely difficult, and he had to be careful with sudden surprise attacks. Herzog manages to take a much more stable shot because he doesn't have to improvise his narration and the danger is far from his setting. This element therefore contributes to the director's overall goal to differentiate the two recordings and their purposes. It can be concluded that Treadwell's intention is to show the world while Herzog's is to act as a response to the environmentalist's intention.

While using an effective method of visualizing, Herzog does an excellent job by keeping the audience's brain processes the "feeling" received as they visualize the scene. To make a film memorable, it needs to evoke a good sense of emotional values from its audience. When the audience feels one or more of these emotions while watching the film: absurd; ashamed; funny; nervous; and sad, this is when the filmmaker knows he or she has succeeded in the show. This film captures not just one, but all of the above. It maintains the constant spike of emotional value throughout the film through Herzog's intentional placement of the footage. He makes sure the transition is either sharp and concise–which sometimes can even be blunt–or smooth in a leisurely manner. In one clip, he chooses to place Treadwell's footage, filled with his joyful expression with the bear then contrasts it with the interview of his colleague's disparaging interview. This is one of the methods used by many filmmakers to push the audience to think critically, thus the scene that has never quite properly explained tends to stick to their mind until the rest of the film. Whereas in the other transition between Treadwell's emotional decline with the bumblebee worker, then to the death of his fox companion's pup, and the remains of baby bear's skull. In this following order, it builds the touching reality of nature not only to Treadwell but also to the audiences as they are watching and exploring alongside him. Possibly, this can be one of Herzog's intentions to sway his audience toward his description of nature as cruel, dissonant, and distorted.

To intensify the emotional value perceived by the audience, Herzog decides to use a variety of points of view to stir opinions. In this film, it doesn't just show a single view point of Treadwell's undying passion to preserve the wildlife, or to show other's absurdity toward him for approaching deadly and wild animals, or even just simply the solemnity of his inner circle who supported his dream. It manages to include all of the different points through the film to make this film credible, relatable, and perhaps even sensible in its own way. Each of the interviews are intended to express a viewpoint in a certain way, thus leading to some criticism of "artificial" or "staged" interviews. Scripted or not, this technique allows Herzog to guide the direction toward his documentary making, and it should have been considered the fact that each person may have different ways to express their sincerity. From my perspective, I have a feeling that it is unusual for them to be filmed in the moment of vulnerability, consequently, leading to their odd and awkward behaviors. The guiding questions–which for the majority of them, the audience is not informed of–are cut off and only present the responder's opinion. Whether that part is considered ethical or lack of transparency, I personally think that it is ambiguous, for it to be actively engaged with the audience. Morality, correct or not, is left to be interpreted, but surely it is effective. Arguably, the most prominent interview that gauges the audience's empathic feeling is when Herzog comforts Treadwell's close friend Jewel Palovak. During that one interview that Herzog first appearance is shown is with his emotional interview with Palovak. It is clearly displayed to the audience that her sentiment has grown big while she respected Treadwell for his environmental advocacy act, and allowed Herzog a chance to listen to the disclosed audio. On the other hand, Sam Egli shows us a rather harsh reality of nature to the audience claiming Treadwell was "asking for it" by deciding to integrate with the bears in the first place. Furthermore, Herzog also adds the viewpoint of many experts such as the Native American community in Alaska, who is "sworn" to the boundary between human and bear for over a century; Biology researcher, who observed and analysed the behavior of the bear for over a decade of his life. Consequently, this not only shows us that Herzog has put in an effort to interview from various sources, but also increases his credibility for not only showing what is emotionally important but intelligent ones as well.
