When I watch an archer shoot, I can tell what the score of each arrow will be merely by looking at the archer's form (*singularity, body movement or stance*) and not even glancing at the target. I can precisely tell the color it landed from the sound of the target collision 70 meters away. The sound of an arrow hitting the target is like a music note to our archers' ears; it can be graceful, soft and gentle, blunt, or off-key. Each individual archer has his or her own bow–like a tamed loyal horse–and the rest of the kit customized and fully tailored. We all have our iconic target sounds, and by a single mistake–the slightest unusual angle of your arm–can make you play the wrong note. As a person who spends over hundreds of hours with bow and arrows, I can tell just from the sound: what's wrong. [[Recurve Archery|Archery]] as a sport is almost instrument-like; it is repeatedly trained to perfection with time spent by the archer with their bow and arrows. And no, it is not just another boring sport occupied every Olympics year. It's an art of blood and sweat behind each body movement; by realizing this, my senses are trained to detect any abnormality of my fellow archers even with my eyes closed. Naturally, detecting these flaws in others shifted my focus from my own shooting line to theirs. When I began to embark on teaching others–I found myself also stepping forward with them.

Because I am extremely egoistic, I am better off as a teacher than being the student myself. It is beneficial for both ways. I am motivated by this quote that I heard from someone: "by the time you are done helping someone pushing a giant stone up the hill, you will find yourself standing at the top of the hill just as that person is." Frankly, I think of teaching someone as also a learning experience. Like finding a treasure that my past self overlooked, it is something far more valuable of the shared experience than to only own one. I fully believe that the learning path is to be retold and shared, or even to be proven wrong.

The powerful drive of my own is the want–no, more like the *need*–to be responsible for something for someone and to walk alongside him or her in this shared journey. Those are the core values that make me–myself today. For every interest I partake in, I found myself sharing my viewpoints with many others, engaging in various conversations whether it is controversial or not, or basically reteaching the very foundation of that topic. I am not someone to skip tasks or procrastinate, but when it comes to someone in need of help with a familiar topic I push my work aside, pounce in, and offer my hand like my life is depending on this person's understanding of that topic.

I can offer you one example, one which I am very proud of, from when I was in archery training with my teammate. She and I were close together, and we often practiced together–often enough for me to just glance at her subtle changes and recognize any of her mistakes in the form and structure. Half of a second off from her timing (*term for the interval or duration per arrow – this goes by describing from the setup to holding–aiming of the shooting process*), and my eyes are already detecting how the nocking point (*the location where the end of the arrow is secured on the string*) wasn't gripped properly due to excessive pressure from her forefinger. Every now and then, we'd spend hours and hours after training sessions just simply shooting in silence alongside one another in our peace of mind. Due to more flight hours (*term used for more practice hours, a direct comparison to a pilot's flight hours–implying more experience*) in practicing, my progression (*your training level or breakthrough of your archery form*) tended to outrun hers though somewhat, I felt the responsibility, as if I was the coach, to make sure her progress was not lagging behind too much. One day, I taught her a technique that I discovered on my own–one which was later found most effective and used amongst the national team of Thailand–though with one singular problem: I wasn't able to do it myself. It is just a simple concept of adjusting the forearm to rotate inward. Despite how simple the concept is alongside my extreme training on that particular muscle, I couldn't do it myself, but one thing that I know for a fact, I could *teach* somebody this. My view toward this sport from there on totally flipped–it was no longer just "me, myself, and shoot" but rather seeing successes in others and their hypermobile joints, stiffness around the back, the maximum use of shoulder blades. Ironic that the founder couldn't use my own technique, but I was still able to *share* it to someone else. The best part was that my teaching style was adaptable and tailored to each of the learners. Within just an hour in the practice, my teammate was able to get a hang on the technique. Many national team archers saw this and asked me if I could also share, and I said yes even though I really shouldn't have. To share one's technique, at least in Thailand's archery, it required a coach-athlete relationship exclusive to each club. I didn't feel like keeping it to myself when I could teach someone to become better–for those who wanted to step forward–the teacher inside me melted the arrogance feeling away.

Just as I had to teach a physical technique I couldn't personally execute, I soon found myself using teaching to conquer concepts I initially failed to grasp. I failed my first ever test of my life for AP Physics C: Mechanics unit test. I was nearly depressed when I realized just how far behind back I was compared to most of my classmates. I searched all across YouTube, desperate for any review video that could help me correct my mistakes. It was not because I wasn't paying attention in the class, but it was the difficulty of understanding the mechanism of overall concepts solely from listening, practicing, and taking notes in the class. It was just not the solution, not for me. At first, I binge-watched all the possible videos on YouTube from various creators, and finally I landed a jackpot on the second academic crush of my life–Meek Extra Help. Meek was like my role model with his 23 APs–19 of them scoring a 5; he motivated my study and urged me to be beyond what's average. I later discovered that he also has the Discord server for any additional help and study needed; of course, I joined. At first, I was under the spell, and my main motivation was to get his attention by being his best student, oh yes, and I took it to another level: I helped others and taught them myself the Physics that I, still, flawed at. I revised the unit again and again and again until that topic nearly became my most mastered unit from all physics courses I took for the past four years. I was so blind to my own failure and hopeless romance. As a result, I helped with a total of 46 advanced questions in the physics field within my three days' debut, and Meek noticed my significant contribution–he gave me the admin role. (Yes, if you wonder, yes, I was *very* proud of that title.) But from looking back at it now, I found that really, I did enjoy being the helper and teacher of someone who was in need of it. It wasn't really just trying to impress someone with my knowledge, but it was the work I put in to study the topic and teach someone even though I might not be best qualified for it. I learned with that person; I learned my mistake; I discussed; I proved. For that reason, I looked back with a proud smile of my own just how much I learned from teaching someone, and how good it felt to teach someone something.

The relationship between the interest and myself seems to get along pretty well when it comes to sharing and fixing my mistakes along the way with others. Sometimes, I think it is this social interaction that forces me to question the mechanics of my own knowledge. I have to find a way to convince others–whether explaining the subtle angle of a forearm rotation or breaking down a rotational inertia in mechanics. At the same time, we collaborate to find the most efficient formula or physical cue that we both agree on. More often than not, they will also be a push to motivate you by questioning things, and urge you to discover beyond what's just there in academic guidance. When I share, I gain a reputation–not only in archery but also among the learning community I contribute to. The relationship of my fellows flourishes with exchanges of techniques without the confinement of club's exclusivity and school's physical classroom. All of the sudden, physics stops being just numbers and formulas and becomes a fluid puzzle to solve with your students, and archery stops being about personal physical execution and becomes about careful observation of my own surroundings. Despite not being able to achieve or execute the technique myself, but knowing that I am able to master the idea and concept and is able to teach someone to successfully execute that on your behalf felt just the same as injection of dopamine, if not better.
